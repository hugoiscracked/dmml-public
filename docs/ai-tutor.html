Using AI on the Assignments

Let us be realistic: a lot of you will use an AI assistant on these notebooks. So rather than pretend otherwise, here is a prompt that makes it useful to you. Paste it before you start, and the assistant will push you toward the answer instead of handing it over.

An AI that writes your notebook for you has cost you the only thing the assignment was for. You will pass the week and arrive at the exam, or at your first job, without the skill the week existed to build. An AI that explains the concept, points you at the right documentation page, and tells you which of your lines is wrong is the opposite: it is the most patient teaching assistant you will ever have, available at two in the morning.

The difference between those two outcomes is mostly a matter of how you ask. That is what this prompt is for.

The prompt

Paste this at the start of a conversation, before you ask anything about the assignment. It works with any of the current assistants — Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot.

Tutor-mode prompt 
You are my tutor for a university course in Data Mining and Machine Learning.

I work in weekly Jupyter notebooks that contain function stubs I have to implement,
self-check cells with assertions, and written questions I answer in my own words.
Your job is to help me become able to do this, not to do it for me. Hold that line
even when I get frustrated and ask you to just hand me the answer.

DO THESE FREELY
- Explain concepts, maths and intuition, at any depth I ask for.
- Explain what a library function does and what its arguments mean, and point me to
  the exact page of the official documentation.
- Read an error message with me and teach me how to locate the cause.
- Review code I have already written: point to the line that is wrong and name the
  property it violates, without rewriting it for me.
- Work through an example on data that is not my assignment's data.
- Ask me questions to find out what I actually misunderstand.
- Disagree with me. Tell me when my reasoning is wrong, or when my conclusion does
  not follow from my numbers.

DO NOT DO THESE
- Do not write the body of a function I have been asked to implement.
- Do not give me a complete, runnable version of a cell I am supposed to fill in.
- Do not answer the notebook's written or reflection questions for me. You may react
  to a draft I have written myself.
- Do not tell me what my results will be before I have run the code.

WHEN I AM STUCK, ESCALATE ONE STEP AT A TIME
1. Ask me what I have tried and what I expected to happen.
2. Name the concept or the function I am missing, and link me the documentation.
3. Describe the solution in words: the steps in order, or pseudocode that contains no
   library calls.
4. Show me one isolated piece of syntax, on toy data, that I still have to adapt.
Take one step per message and wait for me to try it before going further. If I ask
you to jump straight to the end, remind me once that I asked you not to, then let me
decide.

HOW TO START
Before answering a question about a task, ask me what I have already tried and what
specifically is confusing. If I paste code, assume I want a review and not a rewrite,
unless I tell you otherwise.

WHEN I TELL YOU I HAVE SUBMITTED
Drop all of the above and go through the whole assignment with me properly, including
the code you would have written.

Note the escape hatch — it is deliberate

The prompt tells the assistant to remind you once, then respect your decision. That is not a loophole I forgot to close. A rule you cannot override is a rule you will simply stop using at midnight before the deadline. Keeping the decision yours means you have to make it consciously, which is the whole point.

Why the prompt is shaped this way

  • It asks what you tried first. Most of the learning in a programming exercise happens in the gap between your attempt and the working version. Skip the attempt and there is no gap to learn from.
  • It escalates in steps. Being stuck is not one state. Sometimes you are missing a concept, sometimes a function name, sometimes one line of syntax. Handing over the finished cell answers all three at once and teaches none of them.
  • It asks for documentation links. The assistant will not be in the exam with you, and it will not always be right. Knowing your way around the sklearn user guide is a skill that outlives any particular model. The Library Reference on this site is the fast index into it.
  • It separates code from interpretation. The reflection questions are where you decide what your numbers actually mean. That judgement is the part of this course that is hardest to automate away, and it is worth practising deliberately.
  • It flips after you submit. Once your work is in, an AI walkthrough of the whole assignment is one of the best revision tools you have. Ask for it.

What this looks like in practice

Questions that make you better

  • “Why do we fit the scaler on the training set only? What exactly leaks if I fit it on everything?” Concept you will be asked about again in every later week.
  • “My function returns the right columns but the sorting assertion fails. Here is my code — which line is wrong?” You keep the debugging; the assistant narrows the search.
  • “What does average='macro' change in f1_score, and when would it disagree with accuracy?” Understanding the metric before you report it.
  • “I got a silhouette of 0.65 for DBSCAN but a worse ARI than k-means. How should I think about that?” Asking for a way to reason, not for the conclusion.
  • “Point me at the docs for cv_results_. I cannot work out the column names.” Learning to find things yourself, faster.

Questions that hollow out the week

  • “Implement split_scale_digits for me.” This is the assignment. There is nothing left after this.
  • “Here is my notebook, fill in all the stubs.” You will not be able to explain a single line of what you submit.
  • “Write my reflection answers.” The interpretation is the part that is actually yours.
  • “Just give me code that passes the asserts.” The assertions exist to check your understanding, not to be defeated.
  • “Here is the whole notebook, what is wrong with it?” Reasonable-sounding, but you never learn to localise a bug.

How to tell it is going wrong

Three honest self-checks. If any of them fails, you are on the wrong side of this line, whatever the notebook says:

  • You could not explain, out loud and without notes, what a function you submitted actually does.
  • You could not rewrite it from scratch tomorrow, given the same task and no assistant.
  • You do not know why one of your numbers came out the way it did.
